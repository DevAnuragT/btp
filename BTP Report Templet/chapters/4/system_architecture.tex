\chapter{System Architecture and Methodology}
\label{chap:methodology}

This chapter details the architectural engineering undertaken to adapt sequence models specifically for the FreshRetailNet-50K high-frequency stockout task. We strictly follow a logical, story-like progression: beginning with baseline evaluations across major model families, utilizing machine learning explainability to guide our feature curation, engineering custom recurrent networks block-by-block, and ultimately exploring state-of-the-art linear architectures.

\section{Baseline Analysis}

To establish a firm foundation, we formulated a standard 24-hour prediction task utilizing 14 days of historical data. We evaluated foundational architectures across different deep learning families to understand their baseline behaviors on noisy retail data before designing custom solutions.

\subsection{Baseline SimpleRNN}
We began with the most fundamental sequential model: the Simple Recurrent Neural Network (RNN). Configured with a hidden size of 64, this baseline contained 14,744 parameters. While computationally lightweight, it struggled with the vanishing gradient problem over the 14-day sequence, achieving an Hour-Level F1-Score of 0.7750. This indicated that simple temporal routing was insufficient for capturing the complex dynamics of retail supply and demand.

\subsection{Baseline Vanilla and Canonical LSTM}
To address the shortcomings of the SimpleRNN, we implemented a Baseline LSTM. The gating mechanisms (Forget, Input, Output) allowed the LSTM to better retain crucial information from earlier in the 14-day window. Mathematically, the LSTM cell updates its hidden state $h_t$ and cell state $c_t$ at each timestep $t$ as follows:

\begin{align}
    f_t &= \sigma(W_f \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_f) \quad &\text{(Forget Gate)} \\
    i_t &= \sigma(W_i \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_i) \quad &\text{(Input Gate)} \\
    \tilde{c}_t &= \tanh(W_c \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_c) \quad &\text{(Candidate Cell State)} \\
    c_t &= f_t \odot c_{t-1} + i_t \odot \tilde{c}_t \quad &\text{(Final Cell State)} \\
    o_t &= \sigma(W_o \cdot [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_o) \quad &\text{(Output Gate)} \\
    h_t &= o_t \odot \tanh(c_t) \quad &\text{(Hidden State)}
\end{align}

Initially, processing all 13 input features with a hidden dimension of 96, the Vanilla LSTM contained approximately 44,952 parameters, achieving an F1-Score of 0.7852. 

Following our feature reduction phase (detailed in Section 4.2), we refined this into the \textbf{Canonical LSTM}. Standardized on just 6 essential features with a hidden size of 32, this architecture unrolls over the 14-day historical window.

While highly efficient (19,992 parameters, 0.7880 F1-Score), this canonical architecture forces the LSTM gates to simultaneously track both highly erratic demand spikes and strict physical inventory constraints within the same hidden state $h_t$, leading to signal interference.

\subsection{Baseline Transformers}
Given the recent dominance of self-attention mechanisms, we also evaluated heavy Transformer architectures, specifically PatchTST \cite{nie2023patchtst} and Temporal Fusion Transformers (TFT) \cite{lim2021tft}. Despite their massive capacity (over 280,000 parameters), these models underperformed the baseline LSTM, achieving F1-Scores between 0.7540 and 0.7685. The permutation-invariant nature of self-attention proved to be misaligned with the strictly continuous, short-sequence (14-day) physical depletion of retail inventory, suffering from severe over-parameterization.

\section{ML Explainability and Feature Importance}

A fundamental question early in our methodology was whether processing all 13 original features was strictly necessary. Over-parameterization in retail datasets often leads to noise memorization, which explained the poor performance of the Transformers. To investigate this and enhance our recurrent architectures scientifically, we employed ML Explainability techniques—specifically Integrated Gradients (IG) \cite{sundararajan2017ig}—to visualize feature importance over the 14-day sequence.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{../btp-repo/docs/images/ig_heatmap.png}
    \caption{Integrated Gradients Heatmap revealing the disproportionate importance of recent inventory signals compared to historical static features.}
    \label{fig:ig_heatmap}
\end{figure}

As shown in Figure \ref{fig:ig_heatmap}, the explainability heatmap revealed two critical insights that directly shaped our final models:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Recent Dominance:} Features from $t=13$ and $t=14$ (the most recent days) carried exponentially more attribution weight than historical days.
    \item \textbf{Inventory Supremacy:} Raw inventory features (like \texttt{stock\_hour6\_22\_cnt}) completely overpowered noisy demand variables (like historical sales and discounts).
\end{enumerate}

Driven by these explainability results, we aggressively pruned the input space from 13 down to just 6 essential features, providing a much cleaner signal for our subsequent recurrent variations.

\subsection{The Final Custom LSTM: Dual-Stream Inventory Shortcut}

To alleviate the conflicting pressures of the Canonical LSTM and honor the findings from the ML Explainability phase, we engineered our final recurrent variation: the \textbf{Dual-Stream Inventory Shortcut LSTM}. 

We physically separated the 6 optimized features into a Demand Stream (4 features) and an Inventory Stream (2 features), each processed by independent LSTMs. Furthermore, because the IG heatmap proved that the final day's inventory is paramount, we engineered the \textbf{Inventory Shortcut} to route the $t=14$ inventory features entirely around the recurrent layers, directly to the final fusion layer. This prevents the LSTM's forget gates from accidentally diluting the most critical piece of data.

\section{DLinear (Decomposition Linear) Architectures}

Having maximized the potential of recurrent networks, we shifted our methodology to explore modern Decomposition Linear (DLinear) variants. As posited by Zeng et al. (2023) \cite{zeng2023dlinear}, single-layer linear models that decompose time series into Trend and Seasonal components can often outperform complex deep networks on short sequences by entirely avoiding recurrent bottlenecks.

\subsection{Standard DLinear}

Standard DLinear separates the input sequence $X$ into a Trend component $X_{trend}$ (extracted via a moving average kernel) and a Residual (Seasonal) component $X_{seasonal}$. Mathematically, this is expressed as:

\begin{align}
    X_{trend} &= \text{AvgPool1D}(X) \\
    X_{seasonal} &= X - X_{trend}
\end{align}

It then maps these components directly to the 24-hour prediction horizon via independent linear layers ($W_{trend}, W_{seasonal}$), making it fast and interpretably transparent:

\begin{equation}
    Y_{pred} = (W_{trend} \cdot X_{trend}) + (W_{seasonal} \cdot X_{seasonal})
\end{equation}

\subsection{Multi-Kernel DLinear}

To further optimize DLinear for the high volatility of retail stockouts, we engineered the \textbf{Multi-Kernel DLinear}. This architecture utilizes multiple, parallel moving average kernels (e.g., $k=3, 5, 7$) to simultaneously capture short-term panic buying and smoother weekly trends. The trend component becomes a concatenated representation of these multiple resolutions:

\begin{equation}
    X_{trend} = \text{Concat}(\text{AvgPool1D}_{k=3}(X), \text{AvgPool1D}_{k=5}(X), \text{AvgPool1D}_{k=7}(X))
\end{equation}
